P48: Abstract
P49: Distributed onboarding platforms rely on independent agents whose identities must be established before they are given access to protected activities. Weak onboarding controls expose such platforms to impersonation, forged or mismatched identity documents, presentation attacks and duplicate registrations, while manual review is slow and inconsistent at scale. This project developed the Intelligent Agent Identity Verification System, implemented as a web application named Verity, which registers agents, collects document and live selfie evidence and links the verification outcome to platform access. The system combines OpenCV image-quality checks, EasyOCR document-text comparison, ArcFace face matching through InsightFace and Silent-Face-Anti-Spoofing liveness scoring. A Support Vector Machine with a radial basis function kernel combines the six resulting scores into Verified, Manual Review or Rejected decisions, and administrators review uncertain cases. Version 3.1.1 was trained on 7,000 synthetic cases informed by LFW, NUAA and MIDV-500 measurements. A separate 1,500 cases supported development. On 3,000 held-out cases the model achieved 86.07% accuracy, a macro F1-score of 0.8211 and Manual Review recall of 69.83%. With perfect document agreement and liveness, it referred 74 of 75 additional cases with face similarity between 25 and 50 to manual review. Its predecessor verified all 75. Accuracy on the original synthetic test set fell from 76.73% to 67.33%. An earlier hosted demonstration verified one consenting test agent using version 3.1.0, and 46 automated tests passed in an earlier local run. The project demonstrates an auditable verification workflow. Performance on real applicants and the effectiveness of human oversight require further evaluation.
P50: Keywords: identity verification, KYC, biometric verification, liveness detection, facial recognition, access control, support vector machine, distributed onboarding, anti-spoofing

P159: Introduction
P162: Background
P163: Distributed onboarding platforms have become an increasingly common model for organisations seeking to scale customer acquisition across multiple regions without maintaining centralised onboarding teams. By deploying independent agents who operate remotely, organisations can significantly extend their market reach and reduce operational overhead. However, this decentralised model introduces substantial security and trust challenges. When agents operate externally and without direct supervision, the platform's ability to confirm that each registering individual is who they claim to be becomes a critical requirement for maintaining platform integrity and protecting the customers they serve.
P164: The growth of digital onboarding has been accompanied by a corresponding increase in identity-related fraud. Fraudulent activities such as fake registrations, identity impersonation, duplicate account creation, and unauthorised platform access have become increasingly prevalent within distributed digital ecosystems (Experian, 2024). These activities are particularly damaging in agent-based onboarding environments because fraudulent agents interact directly with real customers, exposing organisations to financial losses, reputational damage, and regulatory non-compliance. The increasing sophistication of identity fraud in digital onboarding environments has made automated, intelligent verification frameworks a necessity for platforms operating at scale (Sirangi, 2024).
P165: Traditional approaches to agent verification, which typically involve manual review of submitted identification documents, are inadequate at scale. Manual verification is slow, inconsistent, and prone to human error, and cannot detect spoofing attacks where fraudsters use photographs, pre-recorded videos, or digitally manipulated images to impersonate legitimate individuals during biometric checks (Facia.AI, 2026).
P166: Advances in artificial intelligence and computer vision have produced technologies capable of addressing these challenges. Deep learning-based facial recognition models, particularly ArcFace, have demonstrated significant improvements in face matching accuracy through Additive Angular Margin Loss, which maximises the discriminative separation between facial embeddings (Deng et al., 2019). Liveness detection has emerged as an equally essential component of secure verification, with Chakraborty and Das (2014) establishing its necessity against presentation attacks, and subsequent research demonstrating continued progress in detecting sophisticated attacks including deepfakes (Kuznetsov et al., 2025). Optical Character Recognition systems enable automated extraction and validation of information from identity documents, improving the consistency and efficiency of document verification (Arlazarov et al., 2019).
P167: It is within this context that the Intelligent Agent Identity Verification System was developed. The implemented web application is named Verity. The system addresses the verification and access control challenges faced by distributed agent onboarding platforms by integrating document verification, biometric face matching, liveness detection and a machine learning decision model into a single auditable onboarding workflow.
P170: Problem Statement
P171: Distributed onboarding systems rely heavily on independent agents to register and activate users across different regions. However, the absence of robust identity verification mechanisms exposes such systems to fraudulent activities, unauthorised access, and identity-related attacks. Malicious individuals may exploit weak onboarding controls by submitting manipulated identification documents, using spoofed facial images to bypass biometric checks, or creating duplicate accounts to exploit commission-based onboarding structures. Existing manual verification processes are inefficient, prone to human error, and incapable of consistently detecting increasingly sophisticated fraud techniques at scale.
P172: The increasing sophistication of presentation attacks poses a particular challenge for remote onboarding systems. Fraudsters can bypass facial verification using printed photographs, pre-recorded video replays, and deepfake-generated images, all of which are difficult to detect without dedicated anti-spoofing mechanisms (Kuznetsov et al., 2025). Without liveness detection, a biometric verification step alone is insufficient to confirm that the individual registering is physically present and genuine. Similarly, without OCR-based comparison of document text with registration details, submitted identification documents cannot be consistently cross-referenced, leaving the system vulnerable to identity impersonation through forged or mismatched documents.
P173: Current onboarding platforms also lack an intelligent decision mechanism capable of evaluating multiple verification signals simultaneously. Relying on fixed threshold rules applied to each verification output in isolation increases the risk of both false approvals, where fraudulent agents gain access, and false rejections, where legitimate agents are unnecessarily denied. A learned classification model that evaluates all verification signals collectively could produce more consistent and defensible access decisions (Sirangi, 2024).
P174: Therefore, there is a need for an Intelligent Agent Identity Verification System capable of securely verifying agent identities through document verification, biometric face matching, and liveness detection, and making intelligent, auditable access control decisions within distributed agent onboarding environments.

P197: Justification
P198: The project originated in the context of SalesMesh, a distributed sales and customer-onboarding platform that relies on independent agents working across different regions. Platforms of this kind face a specific and significant risk: because agents operate externally and are not directly supervised, there is no reliable mechanism to confirm that an individual registering as an agent is who they claim to be. Without a structured verification process, such platforms are exposed to fraudulent registrations in which individuals use forged or manipulated identity documents, impersonate legitimate agents or create multiple accounts to exploit commission-based structures. These risks threaten the integrity of the onboarding process, the trust of the organisations using the platform and the safety of the customers being onboarded.
P199: The system addresses these risks by verifying the identity of each agent through document verification, biometric face matching and liveness detection before granting platform access, so that access to onboarding activities depends on verified identity evidence or an administrator's decision. ArcFace significantly improves facial recognition accuracy through additive angular margin loss, making it a reliable foundation for the biometric verification component of the system (Deng et al., 2019). Liveness detection further reduces the risk that agents bypass facial verification using printed photographs, which are a common spoofing technique in remote onboarding environments.
P200: The machine learning decision model adds a further layer of consistency by evaluating all verification signals together rather than applying fixed rules to each in isolation. Its outcomes determine whether an agent is approved, referred for manual review or rejected, while administrators remain responsible for uncertain cases. The system therefore gives distributed onboarding platforms an automated and auditable verification process in which every decision records its basis, supporting a growing number of agent registrations while retaining human oversight.
P203: Scope and Limitations
P206: Scope
P207: The study focuses on the design and development of the Intelligent Agent Identity Verification System. The system supports secure agent registration, document verification, biometric facial recognition, liveness detection, and access control within a distributed agent onboarding context. A machine learning component is included in the form of a KYC Verification Decision Model that evaluates the outputs of the verification pipeline and classifies agents as Verified, Manual Review, or Rejected. The model is trained on a synthetically generated dataset whose feature distributions are empirically derived from established academic benchmark datasets. Registration-time fraud guardrails, covering duplicate national identity numbers, disposable email domains and repeated failed attempts from the same device or IP address, are embedded within the access control module. Authentication supports email and password, Google sign-in and optional passkeys. The platform operates as a web-based system accessible through desktop and mobile browsers.
P210: Limitations
P211: The project encountered several limitations during development and implementation. The accuracy of facial recognition and liveness detection may be affected by poor lighting conditions, low-resolution cameras, image blur, or unclear identification documents. OCR extraction accuracy may also be reduced when agents submit damaged, faded, or low-quality identity documents.
P212: Although the KYC Verification Decision Model adopts a hybrid data acquisition approach in which feature value distributions are empirically derived from established academic benchmark datasets, the model is ultimately trained on a synthetically generated dataset. Its performance may therefore not fully reflect the variability encountered in live deployment scenarios, particularly in cases where real-world verification score distributions differ from those observed in the benchmark datasets used during distribution analysis. The benchmark datasets themselves, while widely used in academic research, were collected under specific conditions that may not fully represent the diversity of document types, camera qualities, and environmental factors encountered in a distributed agent onboarding environment.
P213: The project also depends on third-party open-source libraries including InsightFace, EasyOCR, and Silent-Face-Anti-Spoofing, whose functionality, compatibility and maintenance may change over time. Resource limitations such as the processing requirements of AI models and limited access to large-scale production environments may further constrain extensive testing and optimisation of the system. Furthermore, while the system is designed to reduce fraudulent registrations, it may not completely eliminate highly sophisticated spoofing techniques or emerging presentation attacks beyond those addressed by the selected anti-spoofing model.
P216: Delimitations
P217: The study is limited specifically to agent registration, KYC verification, and access control within a distributed agent onboarding context. The system does not cover customer verification processes outside agent-related onboarding activities. Biometric verification is limited to facial recognition and liveness detection and does not include fingerprint, iris, or voice recognition. The machine learning component is limited to a Support Vector Machine classifier trained on a synthetically generated dataset whose feature distributions are empirically derived from established academic benchmark datasets and does not extend to deep learning models, graph-based fraud detection, or predictive fraud intelligence systems. The empirical distribution analysis conducted in the data acquisition stage is limited to three academic datasets, namely Labeled Faces in the Wild, the NUAA Photograph Imposter Database and MIDV-500, and does not extend to other benchmark datasets or proprietary identity verification datasets. Registration-time fraud signals are limited to duplicate national identity numbers, disposable email domains and repeated failed attempts from the same device or IP address, and the system does not perform post-onboarding behavioural monitoring. The system operates as a web-based platform and does not include a standalone mobile application. 
P220: Literature Review
P223: Introduction
P224: This chapter reviews existing literature related to agent verification, identity authentication, fraud detection and onboarding security in distributed digital platforms. The chapter draws from scholarly articles, industry reports and existing identity verification and fraud detection solutions to establish the knowledge base upon which the Intelligent Agent Identity Verification System was developed.
P227: Current Agent Registration and Verification in Onboarding Systems
P228: Modern distributed onboarding systems rely on independent agents operating across different geographical locations to register, verify, and on-board customers. These systems are commonly used in sectors such as financial technology, telecommunications, insurance, e-commerce, and digital service delivery. The typical onboarding process begins with agent registration, where personal information, contact details and identification documents are submitted for verification before platform access is granted.
P229: Traditionally, agent verification has been conducted through manual review of submitted identification documents by administrators. However, as organizations scale their operations, manual verification becomes increasingly inefficient due to the large volume of agents being onboarded. To address these challenges, organizations have adopted digital Know Your Customer processes that automate identity verification using biometric technologies and artificial intelligence. Modern KYC systems combine document verification, facial recognition, and liveness detection to ensure that applicants are legitimate individuals and physically present during verification (Facia.AI, 2026).
P230: Recent developments in facial recognition technology have significantly improved digital identity verification. ArcFace is a deep learning-based facial recognition model that improves facial matching accuracy through additive angular margin optimization (Deng et al., 2019). ArcFace has become one of the most widely adopted facial verification technologies due to its high accuracy and robustness in identity matching applications. In practical onboarding environments, facial verification typically involves comparing the facial image extracted from a government-issued identification document with a live selfie or video capture submitted by the user.
P231: Alongside facial recognition, liveness detection has become an essential component of secure onboarding systems. Liveness detection as a mechanism is used to determine whether a presented face belongs to a live individual rather than a photograph, pre-recorded video, or other spoofing medium (Chakraborty & Das, 2014). Modern onboarding platforms such as Advance.AI and VerifyID integrate liveness detection with facial verification to strengthen identity assurance and reduce impersonation attempts (Advance.AI, 2026; VerifyID, 2026).
P232: In addition to biometric verification, Optical Character Recognition (OCR) technologies are increasingly used to automate the extraction of information from identification documents. OCR reduces manual data entry and improves verification efficiency by automatically extracting names, identification numbers, dates of birth, and other relevant information from submitted documents. Combined with biometric verification, OCR contributes to a more secure and scalable onboarding process.
P233: Therefore, modern distributed onboarding systems increasingly rely on AI-driven identity verification technologies, biometric authentication, document verification, and liveness detection to improve trust and reduce fraud risks during agent onboarding.
P236: Current State of Image and Video Processing
P237: Image and video processing form the technical foundation of modern identity verification systems. The ability to analyse visual data accurately and efficiently is critical in KYC pipelines, where the quality and integrity of submitted images directly determine the reliability of verification outcomes.
P238: Image quality assessment is a foundational step in document verification pipelines. Before any verification model processes a submitted image, the image must meet minimum quality thresholds to ensure reliable feature extraction. Standard quality metrics including sharpness, brightness, and contrast are commonly assessed using Laplacian variance for blur detection and histogram analysis for brightness and contrast evaluation (Dynamsoft, 2024). Images that fail these thresholds introduce noise into downstream verification models and increase the likelihood of false rejections or false approvals. OpenCV is widely used to implement these assessments because it provides comprehensive support for image processing operations (Dynamsoft, 2024).
P239: Optical Character Recognition has advanced significantly with the adoption of deep learning architectures. Modern OCR engines are capable of extracting structured text from identity documents under varying conditions including different fonts, layouts, and image quality levels. However, OCR output still contains recognition errors that vary with document quality, and these errors are more frequent on damaged, faded or low-resolution documents (Hemmer et al., 2024). This variability makes image quality validation a necessary prerequisite to OCR processing rather than an optional step.
P240: Facial recognition from still images has been transformed by deep learning, with models such as ArcFace establishing new benchmarks for accuracy by learning highly discriminative facial embeddings through Additive Angular Margin Loss (Deng et al., 2019). The process involves detecting and cropping the face region from an image, generating a high-dimensional embedding vector, and computing the cosine similarity between embeddings extracted from two images to determine whether they belong to the same individual. The quality of the input image significantly influences embedding quality, reinforcing the importance of preprocessing before facial recognition is applied.
P241: Liveness detection extends facial recognition to video and live capture contexts by determining whether the presented face originates from a physically present individual rather than a static image or pre-recorded video. Early liveness detection approaches relied on texture analysis and motion-based cues, but these were susceptible to high-quality spoofing attacks (Chakraborty & Das, 2014). Contemporary anti-spoofing research focuses on detecting presentation attacks from single frames and on generalising detection to unseen attack types and capture conditions (Long et al., 2024). Silent-Face-Anti-Spoofing, used in this project, is a lightweight single-frame model of this kind that produces a liveness score for a captured face. The increasing sophistication of video-based attacks, including deepfake-generated facial videos, has driven further research into more robust liveness detection architectures capable of generalising across unseen attack types and capture conditions (Kuznetsov et al., 2025).
P242: Together, these advances in image and video processing form the technical basis for the verification pipeline implemented in this project, where each processing step quality assessment, OCR extraction, face matching, and liveness detection builds upon the outputs of the preceding stage to produce a reliable and auditable identity verification outcome.
P245: Challenges Affecting Agent Registration and Verification
P246: Despite advances in onboarding technologies, distributed onboarding systems continue to face significant security challenges. One of the most common is identity fraud, where malicious individuals use forged or manipulated documents to impersonate legitimate agents. Fraudsters may also attempt to create multiple accounts using different identities to exploit commission-based onboarding systems, leading to financial losses, reduced trust, and reputational damage for organisations.
P247: A further challenge involves presentation attacks, commonly referred to as spoofing attacks. These occur when fraudsters attempt to bypass facial verification systems using photographs, videos, masks, or digitally manipulated images. The increasing sophistication of spoofing techniques has made liveness detection a critical requirement for secure identity verification systems (Kuznetsov et al., 2025). Similarly, it was emphasized that modern face verification systems must incorporate robust anti-spoofing mechanisms to defend against evolving presentation attacks (Long et al., 2024).
P248: The growth of digital onboarding platforms has also increased the complexity of fraud detection. Fraudulent activities are often distributed across multiple accounts, devices, and locations, making them difficult to identify using traditional rule-based verification alone. AI-driven risk scoring and intelligent onboarding frameworks are becoming increasingly important for assessing user trustworthiness and identifying potential fraud risks during digital onboarding processes (Sirangi, 2024).
P249: Scalability presents another challenge. Manual verification processes become increasingly resource-intensive as the number of agents grows. Human reviewers may struggle to process large volumes of verification requests consistently, leading to delays, increased operational costs, and a higher likelihood of human error resulting in both false approvals and false rejections.
P250: The machine learning component of this project relates these challenges to the design of an intelligent agent. In agent terms, the decision model is a learning component: it acquires its decision rules from labelled examples rather than relying solely on fixed threshold rules (IBM, 2025a). In this project, learning takes place only during offline training. The deployed model is static and does not update itself from individual verification cases, because unconfirmed labels could introduce errors or deliberate poisoning. In the context of KYC verification, the model learns the relationships between verification signals, namely face match score, liveness score, OCR agreement and image-quality measures, to produce a classification that accounts for their combined effect.
P251: The reasoning approach underlying the KYC Verification Decision Model in this project is grounded in agentic design rather than in large language models. Agentic reasoning allows AI agents to conduct tasks autonomously by applying conditional logic or heuristics, relying on perception and memory, enabling the agent to pursue goals and optimise for the best possible outcome (IBM, 2025b). In this project, the verification pipeline functions as a goal-based agentic system: each component image quality validation, OCR extraction, face matching, and liveness detection acts as a perception step that feeds into a central reasoning process, which is the trained SVM classifier. The classifier then applies the learned decision boundary to produce a final outcome of Verified, Manual Review, or Rejected.
P252: This design reflects the conditional logic reasoning pattern described in agentic AI literature, where model-based agents use their memory and perception to store a current model of their environment, which is updated as new information is received (IBM, 2025a). The SVM model in this project encodes this reasoning through a margin-maximising decision boundary that separates the three output classes in a high-dimensional feature space. While SVM using a non-linear RBF kernel is less directly interpretable than a linear model, interpretability can be supported through permutation importance analysis, which identifies the verification signals that most significantly influence classification outcomes (Hsieh et al., 2024). This ensures that access decisions remain explainable and auditable, which is important in a KYC context where the basis for rejecting or flagging an agent must be accountable a concern that applies equally to agentic reasoning systems, which can suffer from interpretability challenges when decision logic is not made transparent (IBM, 2025b).
P253: Consequently, the system combines biometric verification, anti-spoofing mechanisms and a learned decision model to address the growing complexity of verification fraud within distributed onboarding environments.

P260: Advance.AI One-Stop Identity Verification Platform
P261: Advance.AI offers an end-to-end identity verification platform called AdvanGuard, designed to help organisations onboard customers securely and efficiently. The platform works by guiding a user through a sequence of automated checks: first, the user submits a government-issued ID document, which the system scans using AI-powered Optical Character Recognition (OCR) to read and extract the text data such as name and ID number without requiring the user to type anything manually. The document is then analysed for signs of tampering, such as edited fields or photocopied images. Next, the user submits a live selfie, which the platform compares against the photo on the ID document to confirm the person registering is the same person on the document. A liveness check is also performed at this stage to ensure the selfie comes from a real, physically present individual rather than a photograph or a digital screen. The platform additionally analyses device signals and behavioural patterns to flag injection attacks or suspicious registration activity. The entire process is automated and returns a verification decision within seconds, significantly reducing the need for manual review by human administrators (Advance.AI, 2026). While AdvanGuard is a comprehensive commercial solution applicable across industries such as banking, fintech, and e-commerce, it does not expose the underlying machine learning decision logic or allow customisation of verification thresholds, which limits its transparency in contexts where explainability of decisions is required. P269: SEON Fraud Detection and Liveness Platform
P270: SEON is a fraud prevention and identity verification platform that takes a distinctive approach by combining pre-verification fraud intelligence with biometric liveness checks. Unlike platforms that begin verification by immediately asking for a document or selfie, SEON first analyses contextual signals about the user before the camera even opens. These signals include the user's email address, phone number, IP address, device fingerprint, and digital history essentially checking whether the person registering has a credible online presence consistent with a real individual. Only after this pre-verification risk assessment does the platform proceed to capture the user's ID document and selfie. During this capture stage, SEON simultaneously detects injection attacks, browser-based spoofing, presentation attacks, and manipulated streams in real time, combining all verification layers into a single risk decision. The platform also has the ability to compare faces across multiple sessions to detect organised fraud rings where the same individual uses multiple identities or the same ID is used by different people (SEON, 2026). SEON's layered approach is relevant to the current project in demonstrating how pre-registration fraud signals can strengthen the overall verification pipeline, which aligns with how this project embeds registration-time fraud guardrails within the access control module rather than treating fraud detection as a separate concern. P278: Facia.AI KYC Verification Platform
P279: Facia.AI is a facial biometrics platform that specialises in liveness detection and face recognition for KYC onboarding. The platform's core capability is its liveness detection system, which determines whether the face presented during verification belongs to a real, physically present person. This is achieved through both active and passive liveness techniques active liveness asks the user to perform a small action such as blinking or turning their head to prove real-time presence, while passive liveness analyses the image in the background for signs of spoofing without requiring any user interaction. Facia's single-frame liveness detection technology ensures that the person being identified is physically present and not using a photograph, deepfake, or other fake representation. The platform also includes a face matching component that compares the live selfie against the photo on a submitted ID document, producing a similarity score to confirm identity. Facia claims particular strength in detecting deepfakes AI-generated or manipulated video and image content even when such content is uploaded directly into the system rather than presented via a live camera. The platform is designed for KYC providers seeking to improve fraud resistance without adding friction to the user experience (Facia.AI, 2026). P325: Gaps in Related Work
P326: The reviewed literature and existing platforms demonstrate significant advancements in identity verification, facial recognition, liveness detection, and KYC decision-making. Platforms such as Advance.AI, Facia.AI, VerifyID, and Joinble provide effective biometric verification and automated KYC services, while the academic studies provide strong technical foundations for facial recognition, liveness detection, and risk-based onboarding decisions.
P327: Despite these advancements, several gaps exist in current solutions. Most commercial identity verification platforms focus on verifying user identities at the point of onboarding but do not provide a transparent, interpretable decision model that can be inspected, validated, or adjusted by an administrator. Many platforms use proprietary black-box AI systems that return a verification outcome without exposing the reasoning behind it, which limits their suitability in contexts where accountability and auditability of decisions are required (Table 2.1). Furthermore, fraud detection platforms and identity verification systems largely operate as separate products, requiring organisations to integrate multiple vendors to achieve comprehensive onboarding security.
P328: Existing academic research also tends to address individual components of the verification pipeline in isolation. Studies on facial recognition, liveness detection, and risk scoring each make important contributions but do not present a unified framework that combines document OCR, biometric face matching, liveness detection, and a machine learning decision model into a single integrated onboarding system. The machine learning models used in existing fraud detection research also frequently depend on large labelled datasets collected from real-world cases, which are difficult to obtain in new or privacy-sensitive onboarding environments.
P329: The Intelligent Agent Identity Verification System addresses these gaps by integrating document verification, facial recognition, liveness detection, and a Support Vector Machine decision model into a single onboarding pipeline specifically designed for distributed agent onboarding environments, where trust, identity assurance, and explainability of access decisions are critical requirements.
P332: Conceptual Framework
P333: The system is based on a conceptual framework that aims to improve the security, reliability, and integrity of agent onboarding within distributed onboarding platforms. The framework begins with prospective agents registering on the platform by creating accounts and submitting their personal information, including full name, email address, phone number, national ID number and date of birth. During registration, agents upload a government-issued identification document and provide a live facial capture through their device camera. The system first performs image quality validation using OpenCV to ensure that uploaded documents and facial images meet the minimum requirements for further processing, assessing blur, brightness, and contrast before any verification step is attempted.
P334: Once the submitted data passes the initial validation stage, the KYC verification process begins. The identification document is processed using EasyOCR to extract relevant information such as the agent's name, identification number, and date of birth, which is then compared against the details submitted during registration to confirm consistency. Simultaneously, the facial image extracted from the identification document is compared with the live facial capture using the ArcFace facial recognition model via InsightFace to determine whether both images belong to the same individual. The Silent-Face-Anti-Spoofing model then assesses whether the selfie shows signs of a presentation attack, such as a printed photograph, and produces a liveness score. Each of these verification steps produces a numerical output score, namely the OCR confidence score, face match score, and liveness score, which are passed alongside the image quality scores into the KYC Verification Decision Model.
P335: The KYC Verification Decision Model is a trained Support Vector Machine classifier that evaluates all verification scores simultaneously and produces a final decision. The model identifies the optimal decision boundary that maximises the margin between the three output classes, classifying each agent into one of three outcomes. Agents whose verification scores fall clearly within the Verified region of the decision boundary are granted full platform access. Agents whose scores fall within a borderline region are classified as Manual Review and routed to an administrator for further assessment. Agents whose combined evidence indicates a mismatch or presentation attack are classified as Rejected and denied platform access. Registration-time guardrails, covering duplicate national identity numbers, disposable email domains and repeated failed attempts from the same device or IP address, operate independently of the model and can block further attempts.
P336: Administrators access the system through a centralised dashboard where they can review verification results, manage agent records, assess manually flagged cases, and view audit logs. Audit logs are maintained throughout the entire onboarding lifecycle to provide accountability and support investigations when suspicious activity is detected. By integrating document verification, biometric facial matching, liveness detection, and an intelligent decision model within a single platform, the system aims to enhance trust and reduce the risk of fraudulent agent registrations, granting access to the onboarding environment only after a Verified decision or an administrator's approval. P349: Introduction

The methodology aims to ensure that the system verifies agent identities, enforces access control decisions and supports secure agent onboarding in distributed environments.
P353: Research Paradigm
P354: The study adopts an applied artificial intelligence and software engineering research paradigm, in which existing AI models and computer vision techniques are integrated into a practical agent identity verification and access control system. It implements and evaluates proven verification technologies for distributed agent onboarding, and develops and trains a Support Vector Machine classification model that evaluates the outputs of the verification pipeline to produce access control decisions.
P357: Data Acquisition
P358: The KYC Verification Decision Model requires a labelled dataset of verification outcomes for training and evaluation. Identity verification data involves biometric information and government-issued identity documents, so collecting real agent verification records is not feasible within the project and would raise significant ethical concerns about consent and data protection. The study therefore adopts a two-stage hybrid approach: an empirical distribution analysis on established academic benchmark datasets, followed by the generation of a synthetic training dataset whose feature ranges are grounded in the observed distributions.
P359: Each verification model was applied to a sample of images from three established academic datasets to observe the score ranges it produces under genuine and fraudulent conditions. No personal identity data was collected or stored; the pipeline components were run on publicly available research datasets and the resulting score distributions recorded. The measurements were recorded once and stored, and later model versions reused them rather than reprocessing the source images.
P360: ArcFace via InsightFace was applied to genuine and impostor face pairs from the Labeled Faces in the Wild dataset to observe the face match score distributions produced for matching and non-matching individuals under real-world conditions (Huang et al., 2007). Silent-Face-Anti-Spoofing was applied to real and photograph-attack face images from the NUAA Photograph Imposter Database to observe the liveness score distributions produced for genuine presentations and printed-photograph attacks (Tan et al., 2010). The Replay-Attack dataset was initially planned for video replay attacks but was not used, so video replay attacks are not represented in the measurements. EasyOCR and OpenCV were applied to identity document images from the MIDV-500 dataset to observe the OCR agreement and image quality score distributions produced across documents captured under varying lighting conditions, camera angles and document quality levels (Arlazarov et al., 2019).
P361: Each generated case contains six scores and an assumed outcome of Verified, Manual Review or Rejected. For version 3.1.1 the generator produced 3,000 Verified, 2,400 Manual Review and 1,600 Rejected training cases. Clear face comparisons used the median and interquartile range of detected same-person LFW scores. Uncertain comparisons included both ordinary and poor image quality. The simulated groups overlap. Benchmark measurements inform score ranges, while scenario frequencies, relationships between scores and outcome labels are modelling assumptions.
P364: Data Preprocessing
P365: Version 3.1.1 used three independently seeded draws. The 7,000 training cases supplied the minimum and maximum of each feature. The same transformation scaled development and test cases, clipping values outside the training range. All six inputs entered the classifier in the order in Table 4.5. Inverse-frequency class weights adjusted the training penalty for errors in each outcome class. The 1,500 development cases supported model selection. The 3,000 evaluation cases were generated after selection.
P368: Model Architecture
P369: The KYC Verification Decision Model is a supervised multi-class classifier at the end of the KYC verification pipeline. It receives the numerical outputs of the preceding verification steps and uses them together to produce a single, interpretable access decision, so no single signal determines the outcome in isolation and borderline cases, where one score is weak but others are strong, are handled more accurately than a fixed threshold system would allow.
P370: The model takes six input features, each a score from one stage of the pipeline. The face match score, from ArcFace via InsightFace, measures the similarity between the facial embedding from the submitted ID document and that from the live selfie. The liveness score, from Silent-Face-Anti-Spoofing, is the model's estimate that the selfie comes from a real, physically present person. The OCR confidence score is derived from the EasyOCR output and measures agreement between the text extracted from the document and the details submitted at registration; it is not EasyOCR's character-recognition confidence. The blur, brightness and contrast scores are produced by OpenCV during image quality validation and together indicate whether the document and selfie meet the minimum quality for reliable verification.
P371: After Min-Max normalisation to the range 0 to 1, the six features form a single input vector to which the model applies its learned decision boundary. It outputs Verified, meaning the combined evidence supports granting platform access; Manual Review, meaning the evidence is uncertain or conflicting and the case is referred to an administrator; or Rejected, meaning the evidence points to a mismatch or presentation attack and access is denied.
P372: The classifier uses a radial basis function (RBF) kernel to learn non-linear relationships among the six inputs. For example, similar face scores can lead to different decisions when document agreement, liveness or image quality differs. LIBSVM combines pairwise classifications and estimates class probabilities from the training data. The Python AI service returns the predicted outcome to the Node.js backend for access control.
P375: Model Implementation
P376: Version 3.1.1 used LIBSVM C-SVC with an RBF kernel, C = 10, gamma = 1 and inverse-frequency class weights. During development the experiment explored scenario distributions, training class allocations and gamma. The revised training examples gave the classifier evidence of uncertain face matches even when OCR and liveness were strong. The fitting procedure used no manually assigned OCR feature weight. Earlier work compared a scikit-learn SVM, an identity-only SVM and a Decision Tree (Pedregosa et al., 2011). Section 5.3.7 distinguishes the training and calibration procedures used in each version.
P379: Model Validation, Testing and Evaluation
P380: The trained models were evaluated on held-out data using accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score. Accuracy measured overall correct classification but was treated as secondary because of the class imbalance. Precision measured, per class, the proportion of predicted positives that are correct, and recall the proportion of actual positives that the model identified. Macro F1, the unweighted mean of the per-class F1-scores, was the primary comparison metric because it balances false positives and false rejections in one measure. Particular attention was given to the Manual Review and Rejected classes, because verifying a fraudulent agent poses a greater risk to platform integrity than referring a borderline case for human review.
P381: A confusion matrix for each model showed where it confuses one class for another, including misclassifications between adjacent classes such as Manual Review and Verified, which bear directly on security.
P382: For version 3.1.1, each input was shuffled separately across 3,000 held-out synthetic cases. Table 5.15 and Figure 5.5 report the mean decrease in macro F1 across 20 repeats and the standard deviation across repeats.
P383: Both SVM versions were evaluated on identical cases within each of two test sets. The revised set assessed the new scenario definitions. The original test partition assessed how well each model reproduced the previous labels. Separate face-score probes examined the frequency of manual review while holding OCR agreement and liveness at their maximum. Chapter 5 reports these measurements separately.

P445: Database Schema
P446: The database schema is the physical PostgreSQL implementation in Supabase. Users stores authentication and role information for agents and administrators; Agents stores personal registration details and links to Users through a foreign key; Documents stores identity document metadata linked to the agent; Verification_Requests records each attempt, linking the submitted document and selfie to the resulting scores; Verification_Scores stores the six pipeline outputs (face match, liveness, OCR confidence, blur, brightness and contrast) for each request; KYC_Decisions stores the SVM outcome per request; Access_Control_Records records the access decision enforced after classification; and Audit_Logs keeps a timestamped record of all attempts and outcomes throughout the onboarding lifecycle. Primary keys identify records, foreign keys relate entities, and normalisation reduces redundancy and maintains integrity.

P464: Authentication and User Management
P465: Supabase Authentication was used to manage user registration, login, session management and role information. The system supports email and password authentication and Google sign-in. Optional passkey (WebAuthn) sign-in was added using the SimpleWebAuthn libraries.
P466: Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning
P467: The AI components of the system were developed using Python and the Flask framework, which provide extensive support for artificial intelligence, machine learning, and computer vision libraries. Python was used to implement the image quality assessment pipeline using OpenCV, OCR-based document verification using EasyOCR, biometric face matching using ArcFace via InsightFace, liveness detection using Silent-Face-Anti-Spoofing, and a Python runtime for the version 3.1.1 LIBSVM decision model. Earlier SVM and Decision Tree comparisons used scikit-learn.
P468: Facial Recognition
P469: The system uses ArcFace (InsightFace) for facial recognition and face matching, through the pretrained buffalo_l model package. ArcFace is a deep learning model that generates facial embeddings and compares facial similarities with high accuracy, making it suitable for identity verification applications (Deng et al., 2019).
P470: Image Processing
P471: OpenCV was used for image preprocessing and quality assessment, including blur, brightness and contrast measurement, document localisation and perspective correction before verification processes are executed.
P472: Optical Character Recognition (OCR)
P473: EasyOCR was used to extract text from identification documents. The extracted name, identification number and date of birth are compared with the details submitted during registration.
P474: Liveness Detection and Anti-Spoofing
P475: The system uses Silent-Face-Anti-Spoofing, with its MiniFASNet models, to estimate whether a submitted selfie shows signs of a presentation attack such as a printed photograph.
P476: Hosting and Deployment
P477: The frontend was hosted on Vercel, the backend on Render and the AI service on Microsoft Azure Container Apps, where it runs in a container under Gunicorn and accepts inference requests only with a server-held API key. Supabase provides authentication, the PostgreSQL database and private storage for identity images.
P480: System Development Techniques
P481: Biometric Verification Technique
P482: The system employs biometric verification by comparing a facial image extracted from a government-issued identification document with a live facial capture submitted by the agent. ArcFace facial embeddings are generated and compared to determine identity similarity scores.
P483: Optical Character Recognition Technique
P484: OCR techniques automate the extraction of information from identification documents. Extracted names are compared by whole name parts regardless of order and capitalisation: every part of the shorter name must appear in the longer name, and at least two distinct parts must match. Identity numbers are compared exactly after normalisation, and dates of birth are compared separately. Matching text does not establish that a document is authentic.
P485: Anti-Spoofing Technique
P486: Two separate liveness mechanisms are used. In the browser, MediaPipe guides the agent through a randomly selected left or right head turn during selfie capture. On the server, the anti-spoofing model analyses the submitted selfie for presentation-attack cues. In addition, from the second verification attempt onwards, document images must be captured through the camera workflow rather than uploaded. These controls restrict the workflow but do not provide general protection against deepfakes, video injection or 3D masks.
P487: Registration-Time Guardrail Technique
P488: Registration-time guardrails identify suspicious registration activity independently of the KYC Verification Decision Model. They block duplicate national identity numbers and disposable email domains, and count failed attempts by IP address and device fingerprint, with default limits of three failures within a 24-hour window. Failed attempts also accumulate strikes, and an administrator can reset an agent's strikes. These are configurable rules; an IP address or device fingerprint does not establish a unique person.
P489: Risk-Based Access Control Technique
P490: Access is granted only when the current decision is Verified. Manual Review cases remain restricted until an administrator approves or rejects them, and Rejected cases do not receive verified-agent access. If the SVM is unavailable but a complete set of valid scores exists, fixed threshold rules provide a fallback decision. Each decision records its basis so that model and fallback outcomes can be distinguished.
P491: Support Vector Machine Technique
P492: SVM classification is the decision technique in the pipeline. Rather than testing each signal against its own fixed threshold, the classifier receives all six scores as one input vector and finds the boundary that maximises the margin between Verified, Manual Review and Rejected. The RBF kernel captures non-linear relationships, so a combination of borderline scores can produce a different outcome from any single score in isolation. Decisions are therefore consistent, data-driven and based on all available evidence rather than rigid thresholds. Permutation importance analysis identifies which signals most influence the boundary, providing an explainable and auditable basis for access decisions.

P575: Training and evaluation data design
P576: The version 3.1.1 experiment generated 11,500 six-score records in three draws. Training comprised 7,000 cases, development 1,500 and final evaluation 3,000. Training deliberately included more uncertain and rejected comparisons than the evaluation mixture. The class quotas support controlled comparisons and do not estimate the prevalence of fraud among applicants.
P577: The generator uses stored local measurements from Labeled Faces in the Wild for face matching, NUAA for real and photo presentation scores, and MIDV document frames and OCR measurements, reusing these historical measurements rather than reprocessing every source image with the current pipeline. The measurement files and their provenance inform score distributions but do not provide joint three-class KYC ground truth; the synthetic labels, relationships between channels and uncertain-case scenarios are modelling assumptions.
P578: The training draw contained 3,000 Verified, 2,400 Manual Review and 1,600 Rejected cases. Development contained 900, 300 and 300 respectively. The final draw contained 1,800, 600 and 600 respectively. Development and final evaluation used separate seeds. Additional fixed-profile face comparisons were analysed separately from these totals.
P579: Only the training draw supplied the scaling parameters and the fitted SVM. The experiment records the generator configuration, seeds, scaling limits, model and class order. The same simulator supplied the held-out cases. Applicants with independently adjudicated identities would be needed to measure performance in the intended setting.
P580: Version 3.1.0 had revised the document scenarios by removing the borderline-OCR Verified scenario and increasing document mismatch to 40% of its Rejected mixture. Version 3.1.1 revised the face scenarios and class allocation. Uncertain face comparisons occur under both ordinary and poor image quality, alongside document mismatch, liveness and recapture scenarios. Learned outcomes can vary across the same face-score band because the classifier uses all six inputs.
P581: Model class probabilities are distinguished from threshold-rule outputs in the decision records and evaluation. A threshold decision may use 0/1 indicator values for compatibility, but these are not calibrated certainty estimates. The model remains static during a verification request; completed cases do not automatically retrain it. Any later retraining requires a separate offline process using appropriately governed and confirmed data.
P582: Retention and recovery
P583: Verified-case images are retained for three business days after the latest successful decision, including manual approval. Business days are Monday to Friday in Africa/Nairobi; public holidays are not excluded. For example, a Friday approval at 14:00 expires on Wednesday at 14:00. Cleanup is checked at startup and every 15 minutes, so scheduled deletion depends on a running backend. Failed removals are retried; signed links must not extend access beyond the permitted window.
P585: The design specifies a maximum retention period of 30 calendar days from upload for each identity image associated with a pending, manual-review or rejected attempt. This period provides a defined opportunity to complete or review the attempt. Status changes and administrative viewing do not restart the image’s retention period. Abandoned uploads without a completed verification request follow the same rule. The separate three-business-day policy applies to verified evidence. Expired images become unavailable for viewing and further processing. Cleanup removes the stored objects and clears their references, with failed removals retried. An unresolved attempt that requires expired evidence must obtain fresh evidence through the disclosure and consent process. Expiry does not confer verified access. 
P586: Verification metadata, scores, decisions, consent records and related audit history are retained for 180 calendar days after attempt closure, meaning a final verified or rejected decision or expiry of an incomplete attempt. Identifying records are then deleted or irreversibly anonymised, and a lawful preservation exception must record its reason, responsible person and review date. Cleanup runs on the same startup and 15-minute schedule, and access checks enforce expiry independently of physical deletion. Recovery copies are restricted to restoration purposes and expire after seven days, and expired-data deletions and preservation decisions are reapplied before restored records become accessible.

P589: The design separates the Vue presentation layer, Express application layer, Flask AI layer and Supabase persistence and identity services, with Supabase providing authentication, PostgreSQL, object storage and database-change events. The hosted allocation, Vercel for the frontend, Render for the backend and Microsoft Azure Container Apps for the AI service, preserves this logical separation; Chapter 5 describes the hosted environment, configuration and available evaluation evidence.
P590: The browser communicates with the application API using JSON or multipart uploads and bearer authentication. Email/password and Google authentication use Supabase Auth. The backend verifies identity, role and object ownership before protected operations. Server-held Supabase and AI credentials are not included in browser code. Camera permission and browser support are prerequisites for the live capture flow; password and Google recovery paths remain available when passkeys are unsupported.
P591: The password sign-in interface uses an application-managed human-check challenge. The backend generates the challenge and validates the submitted answer before password authentication. This is an internal browser-to-backend interface. The interface design includes challenge refresh, clear failure messages and an accessible alternative.
P592: The backend coordinates separate quality, OCR, face, liveness and SVM requests: the first four endpoints consume image data, and the SVM endpoint consumes the six-score JSON vector. A server-held API key accompanies requests to the hosted AI service. The SVM integration uses configurable status and prediction timeouts, selected from cold-start and steady-state measurements together with an overall verification deadline; Chapter 5 records the configured values and observed behaviour. The design requires documented timeout limits, bounded retries and controlled failure responses for each external service.
P593: An unavailable SVM can trigger a logged threshold decision only after a complete valid score vector exists. Failure of the whole AI service before evidence extraction cannot be repaired by classifying missing scores. Such a failure returns a controlled error or incomplete state. Automatic retries of writes must not create duplicate profiles, requests, decisions or access records; the verification plan therefore includes retry checks at each operation boundary.
P594: Files are written to private storage and retrieved as buffers for processing. Authorised administrator image views use signed URLs lasting no more than one hour and, for verified cases, no longer than the remaining image audit window. Socket.io conveys review notifications after database INSERT or UPDATE events place a request into review. Recipient authorisation, reconnect behaviour and consistency with the persisted queue are separate verification concerns. The interface and integration requirements are listed in Table 4.6.

P613: This section presents the design diagrams that show how the system is structured to satisfy the analysed requirements, using the terminology and requirements established in this chapter.
P616: Class Diagram
P617: The class view organises the domain concepts used to describe accounts, agents, documents, verification attempts, processing results, decisions and access. It supports the separation of responsibilities required by FR-07 to FR-13 and NFR-15. The conceptual model provides vocabulary for implementation without requiring every concept to become a separate database table. The class diagram is shown in Figure 4.2.
P620: Figure 4.2 Class Diagram
P621: A verification attempt provides the context for image-quality, OCR, face-match and liveness results. The decision consumes completed evidence, while the access-control record enforces the decision. Agent and Administrator specialise User, whose password is held by Supabase Auth rather than stored as an attribute, and a user may register several passkeys. Audit events refer to the relevant request when it exists, and administrators export them. Ownership is represented through the relationships in the model, while the supporting keys, validation and transaction boundaries are specified in the data design. These controls address orphaned records and partially completed attempts.
P624: Entity Relationship Diagram
P625: The entity relationship view describes the persistent registration, authentication and verification records and the associations between them. It supports DR-01 to DR-05 by linking identity evidence and decisions to the relevant agent and request. Passwords are held by Supabase Auth and are not stored in the USERS entity; passkey credentials and their challenges are recorded in PASSKEYCREDENTIALS and PASSKEYCHALLENGES. The logical entity catalogue in Section 4.4.5 lists the attributes and integrity rules required by the current design. Figure 4.3 shows the entity relationship diagram.
P628: Figure 4.3 Entity Relationship Diagram
P630: An account may exist before an agent profile is completed, so each user has zero or one agent profile. Likewise, a verification request may exist before scores or a decision have been produced. A unique request reference allows at most one score row and at most one decision row; it does not require either row to exist. This distinction lets the model represent pending and failed attempts without requiring a completed result. The same rule applies to access control records, which are created only once a decision has been enforced. References from AUDITLOGS to agents, requests and the acting user are optional, because authentication and blocked registration events may occur before a profile or request exists.
P632: System Architecture Diagram
P633: The architecture view separates presentation, application coordination, evidence inference and persistent records into four logical layers. These layers support the interface requirements in Table 4.6 and allow the verification services to be evaluated independently. Where each layer is hosted does not change its responsibilities.
P634: The Vue.js frontend obtains session tokens from Supabase Auth and sends authenticated HTTPS requests to the Node.js backend, which verifies each token with Supabase Auth before acting on it. The backend sends verification requests to the Python AI service, receives the scores and decision, and stores the results in the Supabase PostgreSQL database. The AI service does not access the database directly; all persistence passes through the backend. Responses return to the frontend through the same interfaces. Two interactions run in the opposite direction: the database notifies the backend of data changes, and the backend sends review notifications to the administrator dashboard. Figure 4.4 shows the resulting architecture.
P637: Figure 4.4 System Architecture Diagram
P639: If a model stage fails, the decision can fall back only on valid scores that already exist; if the AI service is unavailable altogether, the attempt is recorded as an error or left incomplete. Separating the layers allows a component to be replaced behind a defined interface, but a replacement still requires integration testing against that interface.
P641: Sequence Diagram
P642: The sequence view traces a verification attempt from evidence submission to a recorded outcome, including administrator review where required. It supports FR-07 to FR-16 and FR-24. Figure 4.5 shows the sequence diagram for this flow.
P645: Figure 4.5 Sequence Diagram
P647: The backend calls each AI endpoint in turn and stores its result against the verification request. A failed quality check ends the attempt before OCR, face matching or liveness detection run. Once all scores are stored, the backend requests classification; if the classifier is unavailable, a threshold fallback is applied to the existing scores and recorded as the decision basis. Access is granted only for a verified outcome, so a manual-review result withholds access until an administrator, after a role check, records a final decision. Each significant step is written to the audit log.

P810: Request status values distinguished pending, verified, review and rejected requests. The decision enumeration excluded pending because it represents completed classifications. The decision_basis field distinguished SVM classifications from placeholder threshold decisions, which allowed stored outputs to be interpreted correctly when model inference was unavailable. Row-level security was enabled in the base schema with policies supplied in separate scripts, and the backend independently validated bearer tokens through Supabase and checked roles before protected actions.
P811: The inspected documentRetentionService.js considers the latest request for each agent and skips it unless its status is verified. For eligible verified cases, the audit window expires three business days after the successful decision; business days are Monday to Friday in Africa/Nairobi, without public-holiday exclusions. The job runs at startup and every fifteen minutes while the backend is running. It removes eligible image objects and clears file references while retaining document rows, scores, decisions and audit history. Failed removals leave references available for a later cleanup attempt. This describes the source; live deletion scheduling and object removal have not been demonstrated.
P812: The amended DR-08 instead requires pending, review and rejected images, including abandoned uploads, to expire 30 calendar days after upload without the deadline restarting on viewing or status change, and verification metadata, scores, decisions, consent records and related audit history to expire 180 calendar days after attempt closure unless a documented lawful preservation obligation applies (Section 4.2.3). These are proposed project periods, not statutory retention periods.
P813: The verified-only purge does not implement the 30-day policy, abandoned-upload expiry, the 180-day metadata lifecycle or the preservation-exception record required by DR-07 and DR-08, nor the NFR-06 rule that signed URLs must not outlive the applicable image deadline. Expiry enforcement for non-verified evidence and safe reapplication after restoration remain implementation and testing gaps.
P815: Application Modules
P816: Authentication, roles and passkeys. Authentication middleware rejected missing or invalid bearer tokens, and role middleware restricted administrator operations. Email/password and Google sign-in were supplemented by WebAuthn passkeys using SimpleWebAuthn. The public.passkeys table stored the credential identifier, public key, signature counter, transports, device type, backup status, label and device metadata; the application did not receive device fingerprint or face templates from WebAuthn. Challenge and rate-limit records were stored separately. Client support detection controlled which passkey actions were offered. WebAuthn relying-party, origin and fresh-challenge validation are distinct from the HTTP CORS policy (World Wide Web Consortium, 2021).
P817: Human check. The internal captcha.js utility generates a five-character image challenge, stores its answer in process memory and sets a five-minute lifetime. The first validation attempt consumes the challenge, whether the answer is correct or incorrect. authController.js checks the CAPTCHA before calling password authentication and returns missing/invalid-challenge feedback. LoginPage.vue provides a refresh button and a labelled input. No external CAPTCHA provider is integrated.
P818: A process restart loses outstanding challenges, and separate backend processes do not share the in-memory challenge map, so a challenge generated on one instance may fail on another unless routing or a shared store prevents it; neither arrangement was verified. The image description and refresh control provide no non-visual solution, and Google sign-in and optional passkeys do not establish an equivalent alternative for every password user. The accessible-alternative part of IR-11 and restart and multiple-instance behaviour remain untested.
P819: Registration and evidence capture. Agents entered personal details and supplied document and selfie images through the capture interface. Server upload middleware accepted JPEG and PNG MIME types and imposed a 5 MiB limit per file, returning a validation message for unsupported or oversized files. Registration logic checked existing national identity numbers. The storage integration used a private Supabase bucket; its deployed access settings were not live-tested in this update.
P820: Capture rules. The name prefilled from Google sign-in was made editable because a Google account name may differ from the official name on the identity document. The first submitted attempt could use supported document uploads, while the backend required camera capture for the second and later attempts, determining earlier attempts from stored verification requests so that the rule survived page refreshes; a quality-failed attempt also counted. The capture-source check restricts the workflow but is not cryptographic proof that an image came from a camera. During selfie capture, MediaPipe in the browser asked the agent to turn their head to the left or right, chosen at random, after the blink and open-eye challenges were removed. This browser check is separate from the server-side anti-spoofing score.
P821: Disclosure and consent. The inspected frontend, backend and database files contained no pre-collection consent control, no record of account, purpose, notice version and server timestamp, no backend enforcement of that record and no withdrawal handling, all of which amended DR-09 requires where consent is the approved processing basis. Account registration and image submission do not meet this requirement, and both the controls and the applicable processing basis remain unresolved; a proposed notice alone does not close DR-09.
P822: Verification pipeline. Four services produced the evidence signals. The quality service computed sharpness as Laplacian variance, brightness as the mean HSV value channel and contrast as the grayscale standard deviation, and images below the configured thresholds were rejected with specific messages before further processing. The OCR service compared the extracted name, identity number and date of birth with the registered details. Face matching compared document-portrait and selfie embeddings generated with the ArcFace implementation in InsightFace (Deng et al., 2019), and liveness scoring used the Silent-Face anti-spoofing models. These were evidence signals: neither text agreement nor facial similarity alone proved that a document was authentic.
P823: The quality service located the document in the image and corrected its perspective, so that a desk, background or booklet cover did not dominate the measurements, and it applied separate thresholds to documents and selfies. Some thresholds were made deliberately permissive during cross-phone capture testing; they are implementation defaults rather than experimentally established acceptance limits. OCR processing used document-type templates, front and back evidence, date parsing, identifier normalisation and machine-readable-zone processing where applicable. Name matching compared whole name parts regardless of order and capitalisation: every part of the shorter name had to appear in the longer name, at least two distinct parts had to match, and partial-word or single-name matches were insufficient. This replaced an earlier substring approach that produced inconsistent matches. Identity numbers were compared exactly after normalisation and dates of birth separately. Face matching used the pretrained InsightFace buffalo_l models, selected the largest face where several were detected, and reported missing faces or model-initialisation failures rather than treating them as successful comparisons. Liveness scoring used the MiniFASNet model resources and returned a score between 0 and 1.
P824: The backend sent the evidence to the quality, OCR, face and liveness endpoints and assembled six numerical features, defined in Table 5.5. Raw image-quality measurements were kept in their original units, and MinMax scaling was applied inside the model processing path. Missing, non-finite, wrongly typed or out-of-range evidence was rejected rather than converted to zero.

P844: Exception Handling and Decision Provenance
P845: At the AI endpoint, an unavailable or inconsistent model bundle produces an unavailable-service response. At application level, complete valid scores may enter threshold fallback when SVM inference is unavailable or unusable. The decision basis is placeholder_threshold, and the rule outputs are not calibrated probabilities. The fallback rejected a case if the face score was below 50, liveness below 0.50, OCR agreement below 0.40, or blur below 80 together with a face score below 65. Otherwise, it verified a case only if the face score exceeded 80, liveness exceeded 0.75 and OCR agreement exceeded 0.70, and it sent all other cases to Manual Review. These application paths are outside the model-only results in Section 5.4.3.
P846: In verificationService.js, SVM_TIMEOUT_MS defaults to 5000 milliseconds, can be overridden by the environment and applies through separate AbortSignal timeouts to the status request and then the prediction request; it is not a five-second deadline for the complete workflow. The deployed value, an overall verification deadline and a uniform bounded retry policy were not verified, and no longer timeout, warm-up schedule or minimum-replica arrangement is claimed as configured.
P847: The source distinguishes a status-request transport failure, a reported unavailable model, a prediction transport failure, a prediction HTTP error and an invalid prediction. A non-successful status response can be reduced to modelLoaded: false, so the resulting model-unavailable reason does not by itself distinguish every upstream status error. Diagnosis requires the status/prediction response details and service logs, with cold and warm measurements recorded separately.
P848: accessControlService.js attempts to append KYC_DECISION_MADE with details.svmFallback = true, details.reason, the request and agent context and a system actor, but auditLogger.js may return null if insertion fails, so a logging attempt does not prove that the reason persisted. The four historical placeholder decisions would need matching audit rows and backend and AI logs joined by request identifier and time; these were not available, so their failure causes and hosting environments remain unknown, and they are not attributed to Azure cold starts.
P850: Dataset Description and Preparation
P851: Version 3.1.1 used 11,500 synthetic cases generated in independent training, development and evaluation draws. Every case contained the six features in Table 5.5 and a scenario-derived label. The original v3.1.0 test partition was reused separately for comparison and is excluded from the 11,500 total.
P852: The generator reused stored benchmark measurements (1,000 LFW face pairs, 1,200 NUAA presentations, and 500 MIDV quality frames with MIDV OCR measurements), whose summary statistics and file hashes are recorded in the machine learning README and dataset metadata. These measurements informed the score distributions; the source images were not reprocessed for versions 3.0.0, 3.1.0 or 3.1.1, and none of the benchmark sources supplied the three-class KYC labels.
P853: Face and liveness values were sampled from truncated distributions informed by the recorded measurements; OCR and image-quality values combined measured statistics with explicit simulation assumptions, using a multivariate distribution over transformed blur, brightness and contrast for quality. Some unresolved and genuine scenarios deliberately shared observed distributions to represent information absent from the six features, so the labels reflect assumed latent scenarios rather than a threshold applied to the observed scores.
P854: Clear face comparisons were sampled using the median and interquartile range of detected same-person LFW scores. The generator also produced uncertain comparisons under ordinary and poor quality. Training included document mismatch, liveness and recapture scenarios, allowing conflicting scores to occur. Section 4.2.3 describes the relationship to earlier label assumptions.

P861: Model Training, Selection and Packaging
P862: The version 3.1.1 classifier used LIBSVM C-SVC with an RBF kernel, C = 10 and gamma = 1. Min–max scaling was fitted on 7,000 training cases. Each class weight equalled 7,000 divided by three times its training count. The error penalties were 0.7778 for Verified (3,000), 0.9722 for Manual Review (2,400) and 1.4583 for Rejected (1,600). All six inputs entered the fitted classifier without hand-assigned feature weights.
P863: For version 3.1.0, the grid selected C = 10 and gamma = 1, with a training cross-validation macro F1 of 0.710958. Sigmoid calibration was fitted on the 1,500 validation rows, and prediction takes the class with the highest calibrated probability; the saved model carries no review multiplier or verification threshold. The identity-only SVM, using face, liveness and OCR only, selected C = 100 and gamma = 1 (cross-validation macro F1 0.706363), and the comparison Decision Tree selected a maximum depth of 5 and a minimum leaf size of 20 (0.699150). The tree and the identity-only SVM are comparison artifacts; the service loads the full six-feature SVM.
P864: Version 3.1.1 used LIBSVM probability estimation during training. The separate development draw supported choices about the scenario mixture, training allocation and gamma. After selection, both versions were scored on the same original rows and the same revised draw. Changing both the labels and class mix limits attribution of the results to any single change.
P865: The earlier version 3.0.0 model was produced by improve_model.py. That experiment compared raw and transformed quality inputs, native multiclass and one-versus-rest SVM variants, and sigmoid, isotonic and temperature calibration, using separate 750-row calibration and selection partitions. It selected the raw_native_sigmoid configuration with C = 10 and gamma = scale (training cross-validation macro F1 0.672766) and a probability policy with a review multiplier of 1.0 and a verification threshold and margin of 0.0, and it passed a release gate against version 2 on a shared fresh sample. These facts describe version 3.0.0 only.

P974: Discussion
P975: The implementation combined evidence capture, numerical scoring and persistent verification decisions. The live exports supported this path through 12 request and score pairs and nine matching decisions, and the automated tests added evidence for selected interfaces, input handling, passkeys and retention. However, three attempts remained pending, and the exports did not establish access enforcement, administrator intervention or user acceptance. Deployment was completed across Vercel, Render and Azure, with public availability checks on 27 October 2026, and the hosted run on 5 November 2026 showed one Verified decision being stored, granting access and being written to the audit log.
P976: The revision addressed a specific error pattern. With perfect OCR and liveness, v3.1.0 verified all 75 borderline face comparisons in the held-out profiles. Version 3.1.1 referred 74 and verified one. Its higher accuracy on the revised test cases came with lower accuracy on the original cases and more genuine-labelled cases referred to review. P977: Decision provenance distinguished SVM outputs from threshold-rule outputs in the live records. Token validation, role checks, guardrails and image retention were implemented, but the exports did not include access-control or audit records that would prove their live enforcement. The successful isolated passkey and retention tests supplied narrower evidence for those modules; no general security or privacy-compliance conclusion was drawn.
P979: Limitations
P980: Data validity was the main limitation. The labels were synthetic, the class quotas were fixed and the score relationships were assumed. The test partition came from the same simulator and not from independent real applicants, and the benchmark measurements were reused from earlier runs, so later changes to the image pipeline could cause a mismatch between the source distributions and deployed scores.
P981: Evaluation coverage remained limited. The live evidence comprised 12 requests from eight agent identifiers, with no independent identity labels or recorded model versions, so it could not establish real-world accuracy. No demographic fairness study, presentation-attack campaign, camera comparison, participant usability study or timed ten-run pipeline benchmark was documented. The 750-row calibration and selection partitions of version 3.0.0 left a risk of policy overfitting; version 3.1.0 calibrated on 1,500 validation rows and had no separate policy-selection partition. Model metrics excluded the full effects of capture quality gates, fallback and administrator intervention. The hosted system was demonstrated with one successful run only, with the paths listed in Section 5.4.2 left unexercised, and its 13.6-second decision time is a single observation rather than a measured latency, concurrency or availability result.
P982: Retention and informed collection remain incomplete against the amended design. The verified-only cleanup and its passing local tests do not demonstrate the DR-08 lifecycle described in Section 5.3.2, and the DR-09 consent control, backend record and withdrawal route were not found (Section 5.3.3), so DR-08 and DR-09 remain unresolved. Unpinned image-processing dependencies and the absence of a historical environment snapshot also limit exact reproduction of the complete pipeline.
P983: Recovery and integration verification remain incomplete. Restoration of the daily Google Drive backups has not been tested, and their protections remain unverified (Section 5.2.3). The SVM five-second value is a per-call source default, not a confirmed deployed setting or overall deadline. Matching reasons for the four historical fallback decisions, cold and warm timings and effective cloud settings remain unavailable, and the extended C4-T18 and C4-T24 scenarios are untested.
P984: IR-11 and IR-12 are not fully demonstrated. The internal CAPTCHA lacks an established accessible alternative and verified restart and multiple-instance behaviour, and the actual production HTTP and real-time origin settings remain unverified (Section 5.2.2). C4-T25, C4-T26 and C4-T27 are untested, and no full accessibility-conformance claim is made. Real-device passkey sign-in on the hosted domain was not confirmed after the relying-party configuration was corrected.
P985: Data protection review. The design uses Kenya’s Data Protection Act, 2019 as the reference for notice, lawful processing, consent, retention and security, including sections 25 and 29-32, 39-41, sensitive-data processing under sections 44-45 and overseas transfers under sections 48-49 (Republic of Kenya, 2019). No supervisor or institutional decision confirming the processing basis, biometric-processing safeguards, impact-assessment outcome or overseas-hosting safeguards is recorded. These review outcomes remain unverified; neither hosting nor a proposed consent notice establishes legal compliance.
P986: Outstanding application work, separate from this document, is to implement the non-verified-image and metadata lifecycles, applicable consent and withdrawal controls, an accessible human-check alternative, production origin separation and the recovery procedure, and to select any SVM timeout or replica and warm-up changes only after measurement. Outstanding evidence includes the remaining deployed service settings, the Google Drive backup script and execution records, verification of backup protections and retention, an isolated-restoration result, matching historical fallback audit and log records, execution results for the extended scenarios and the institutional review outcome. None is treated as completed by editing this chapter.
P987: The evidence supports a functioning implementation and a reproducible synthetic classifier experiment. The real-world effectiveness of the classifier remains unestablished. Conclusions, recommendations and future work, including periodic retraining on administrator-confirmed decisions, are presented in Chapter 6.
P990: Conclusions Recommendations and Future Work
P992: Introduction
P993: This project addressed the need to assess an agent's identity during registration and connect the verification outcome to controlled access, combining document evidence, facial comparison and liveness assessment in an accountable workflow. It developed and hosted a system that captures identity evidence, produces six verification scores, classifies cases and provides an administrator review route. The findings establish a functioning implementation and a reproducible synthetic classifier experiment; they do not establish that the system can reliably approve real applicants without human oversight. This chapter draws conclusions against the five project objectives, recommends actions arising from the reported limitations and identifies priorities for further investigation.
P995: Conclusions
P997: Investigation of Current Registration and Verification Practice
P998: The investigation of registration and verification practice provided the basis for the system requirements. As recorded in Section 5.6.1, it was addressed through the Chapter 2 literature review and carried into Chapter 4, defining a workflow from account registration and evidence collection to a verification decision and administrative handling, which the completed implementation connects. The evidence does not establish a measured reduction in processing time or staff workload compared with an existing registration process.
P1000: Identification of Challenges and Fraud Risks
P1001: The identified verification challenges informed the image-quality gate, document-text comparison, facial matching, liveness scoring and registration guardrails. These countermeasures were implemented, but their presence does not establish their effectiveness against real fraud: on the revised v3.1.1 test set the classifier verified 25 of 600 Rejected cases and 132 of 600 Manual Review cases, so combining the available scores leaves consequential errors unresolved. The objective informed the system's controls, while the evidence supports continued human oversight and does not establish a real-world fraud-prevention rate.
P1003: Examination of Existing KYC Platforms
P1004: The examination of existing know-your-customer platforms supplied the related-work comparison and research gap in Chapter 2, as summarised in Table 5.17. It provided context for development rather than a comparative product trial, and Chapter 5 contains no matched operational evaluation against those platforms. The implemented workflow is therefore the project's response to the identified requirements, with no claim that it is more accurate, faster or more secure than existing KYC products.
P1006: Development of the Identity Verification System
P1007: The frontend, application backend, AI service and persistent records were implemented and hosted across Vercel, Render and Azure, with Supabase providing authentication, database and storage services. The live exports contained 12 verification requests linked to complete six-score records and nine decisions whose outcomes matched the request status, supporting the path from evidence scoring to stored decisions. Source inspection established administrator review, access-control and audit mechanisms, but live enforcement was demonstrated for only one case: the hosted run on 5 November 2026 verified a consenting test agent with the version 3.1.0 SVM, granted access and recorded each step in the audit log.
P1008: The updated classifier is a six-input RBF SVM trained on 7,000 synthetic cases, with a separate 1,500 cases for development. On the revised 3,000-case test set it achieved 86.07% accuracy and macro F1 of 0.8211. On the original test set its accuracy fell to 67.33%, compared with 76.73% for v3.1.0. The revision better matched the new review labels while sending more originally Verified cases for manual assessment.
P1009: The development objective was achieved for the core implemented and hosted workflow. Full coverage of the amended requirements remains incomplete, particularly for informed collection, non-verified image retention, metadata expiry, recovery and integration controls. Those omissions limit any claim of operational readiness.
P1011: Testing and Validation of Effectiveness
P1012: The testing objective was partly met. The local evaluation passed 46 automated tests covering selected classifier contracts, decision handling, passkeys, retention and frontend behaviour; public endpoint checks established hosted availability; and one recorded hosted run completed the workflow from capture to access grant. However, three exported requests remained pending, no Manual Review decision appeared in the live sample, and the records lacked independent identity labels, so real-world accuracy and complete workflow reliability are not established.
P1013: Manual Review was the weakest classifier outcome, with recall of 69.83%, and 157 of 1,200 synthetic non-Verified cases were incorrectly verified. The evaluation therefore does not support autonomous approval, and the effectiveness of administrator intervention remains unmeasured. No representative participant usability study, demographic analysis, comprehensive security assessment or timed hosted pipeline benchmark was reported, and passing selected local tests does not resolve those gaps.
P1015: Project Contribution and Boundaries
P1016: The project contributes an integrated identity-verification implementation and a reproducible method for assessing a classifier that combines document and biometric scores. It preserves a distinction between model and threshold decisions and reports consequential classification errors alongside aggregate accuracy. Its strongest evidence concerns selected local software behaviours, stored request-to-decision relationships and model performance under the specified simulator assumptions. The findings remain bounded by synthetic labels, a fixed class mixture, a small live sample and incomplete operational verification. Hosting establishes availability; it does not establish safety, legal compliance, scalability or effectiveness across real applicant populations.
P1018: Recommendations
P1019: The following actions are prioritised by their importance to responsible use. They address findings and unfinished requirements reported in Chapter 5 and do not claim that the outstanding controls have been completed.
P1021: Retain Human Oversight and Restrict Operational Use
P1022: The adopting organisation and system administrators should restrict current use to controlled evaluation and demonstration. Before any trial affects real access rights, they should establish authorised human review of proposed outcomes and verify that decisions, access records and audit events agree. Review must cover proposed Verified outcomes as well as the Manual Review queue, because 132 of 600 Manual Review cases in the revised v3.1.1 test set were classified as Verified. Human intervention is an essential safeguard, but its ability to correct errors must itself be evaluated before wider adoption.
P1024: Complete Informed Collection and Retention Controls
P1025: Before further operational collection of identity evidence, developers and the adopting institution should resolve the notice, applicable consent, withdrawal and processing-basis gaps identified in Section 5.6.3. Developers should complete and test the amended retention requirements for non-verified images, abandoned uploads, metadata and preservation exceptions, including access expiry and deletion retries. The verified-only cleanup tests do not establish these behaviours. The proposed 30-day image and 180-day metadata periods should remain subject to institutional approval, together with biometric-processing and overseas-hosting safeguards; they should not be presented as statutory periods or evidence of compliance.
P1027: Verify Recovery and Production Controls
P1028: System maintainers should inspect the daily Google Drive backup process and demonstrate an isolated restoration before relying on it, covering encryption, restricted access, the proposed seven-day expiry, backup contents, model and configuration recovery, record integrity and reapplication of expired-data deletion, because backup creation was reported but restoration was not tested. Developers should also complete production-origin separation and an accessible human-check alternative, then execute the outstanding failure, origin, consent and human-check scenarios; source inspection and health responses are insufficient evidence of deployed behaviour.
P1030: Establish Operational Evidence Before Optimisation
P1031: Developers and maintainers should record the deployed revision, model version, effective configuration and persisted decision provenance for each evaluated run, investigate incomplete requests and link fallback decisions to their actual failure causes. Chapter 5 could not determine why four historical decisions used threshold fallback, so infrastructure changes should not be justified by an assumed cold-start problem. The planned cold and warm timing measurements, access-control checks and administrator walkthroughs should come first, and changes to timeouts, replicas or service tiers should depend on measured bottlenecks and agreed acceptance criteria. Optional performance tuning should follow the essential safeguards above.
P1033: Future Works
P1034: The following research directions are presented in priority order and extend the evaluation beyond the completed study; implementing and testing the outstanding Chapter 4 requirements remains current completion work under Section 6.3. The scope, resources and feasibility of each extension should be agreed with the supervisor before it begins.
P1036: External Validation of the Complete Verification Pipeline
P1037: The highest priority is to evaluate whether the synthetic findings transfer to real applicants. Researchers would require appropriately authorised identity samples, independently adjudicated outcomes and representative coverage of document types, cameras, capture conditions and relevant applicant groups. The principal change would be evaluation of the full capture-to-decision workflow against real ground truth, rather than generated score vectors. A held-out external set should measure per-class performance, both false-verification categories, calibration and subgroup uncertainty. Authorised presentation-attack testing should assess the liveness component separately. Success would depend on predefined error tolerances and consistent performance across the evaluated conditions, rather than aggregate accuracy alone.
P1039: Evaluation of Review Policies and Periodic Retraining
P1040: The remaining Manual Review recall of 69.83% and the 132 review cases verified by version 3.1.1 justify a controlled investigation of decision policies and model updating. Researchers should compare the existing model with recalibrated or more conservative review policies using the same development data and an untouched evaluation set. Required resources include sufficient adjudicated cases, documented error costs and a manageable administrator review capacity. Administrator-confirmed decisions could support periodic retraining after independent label checks; they should not be assumed correct merely because an administrator approved them. Version 3.1.1 was itself an offline retrain under changed labelling assumptions, not an online update from administrator decisions. Evaluation should report false verifications, genuine-case rejection, review workload and calibration, with uncertainty estimates. Promotion should require a justified improvement at an acceptable review burden, and retain a versioned rollback path.
P1042: Longer Studies of Usability and Operational Reliability
P1043: After the essential controls and baseline checks are completed, a longer supervised study should examine how applicants and administrators use the system over time. This would require representative participants, an isolated or appropriately approved deployment, accessible task scenarios and monitoring across realistic workloads. The extension would assess sustained use and human decision-making, which were not measured in the present study. Outcomes should include task completion, assistance and recapture needs, accessibility barriers, administrator correction of errors, and cold and warm response times. Longer observation should also measure failures, recovery and changes in score or decision distributions. These results would determine whether the workflow remains usable and dependable beyond a short demonstration and whether broader deployment is justified.
